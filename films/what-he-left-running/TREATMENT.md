> **Runtime 3:43 total** — 3:40 of picture (beats 1–12) plus a 0:03 end card.
> Twelve beats. Turn at 2:24. **Speech-free ≥ 44s — 20% of the 220s of
> picture.** The denominator is picture, not the 223s total; the card is
> excluded from numerator and denominator alike. Excluding it from one and not
> the other is what produced the earlier "45s" figure, which was 20% of the
> total measured against a picture-only numerator.
>
> The card is counted, not excluded. Beat 12's nineteen-second hold on her face
> is not shortened to absorb it. Both figures clear the festival's 3:00 floor

> the film · the father is never heard · one house, four times of day.

> **"Silence" in this document always means speech-free, never signal-free.**

## Structural notes

**Speech-free budget, measured against the stage-4 script:** beat **1** (24s,
whole) · beat **6** (14s, partial) · beat **10** (25s, whole) · beat **12** (7s,
partial, from the 3:33 mix drop to the cut) = **70s of 220s picture — 31.8%**,
against a 44s floor. Two of the four are partial beats, unlike the sister film
where every speech-free beat is a whole one; the per-beat seconds in
`LOOK-AND-SOUND.md` are the machine-readable record and this paragraph is
derived from them, not the other way round. Measured for real at stage 8.

**Why the film opens on a kettle in an empty room.** It establishes the entire
premise — a system, running, alone, being kind on schedule — in twenty-four
seconds with no exposition, no interface, and no person. If the audience
understands the film's world from a kettle, every subsequent explanation is
unnecessary, which is what licenses the rest of the discipline.

**Why the turn is delivered flat.** The house has no stake and performs none.
The most devastating available delivery of *he knew and did nothing for six
years* is a machine stating it with an as-of date. Any performance of gravity
would let the audience off — they would receive the emotion instead of doing it.

**Why we never hear the unedited version.** The audience has spent 3:40 wanting
to hear the father. Giving them the moment where she finally can, and then
denying it, moves the film's subject from *what he was like* to *her decision to
find out* — which is the only part she owns. It is also the only ending that
respects the sovereignty argument: the choice is the film, not the content.

**Why 4:3.** The film is about a house closing in and a person inside it. The
narrow frame does that work for free, it is a formal signature in a festival of
2.39:1 entries, and it is the aspect ratio nobody chooses when a model will
happily give them scope.

**Why handheld only once.** Every other shot in the film is locked. Beat 10 is
the only time she moves through the house as a person rather than an executor,
and it is the only time the camera has a body. One deviation, load-bearing.

## Language check — the beats most at risk

Three places where this script will drift toward slop if it is not watched:

- **Beat 5**, "what did he say about me" — the pull toward a speech about
  regret. The house declines in eleven words and the scene is over.
- **Beat 9**, the turn — the pull toward the house explaining the moral weight
  of what it did. It states a fact and a date. Nothing else. If a single clause
  of interpretation survives into the shooting script, the film is lost.
- **Beat 12**, the ending — the pull toward a final line that means something.
  It says four words, they are practical, and they are not about her.
