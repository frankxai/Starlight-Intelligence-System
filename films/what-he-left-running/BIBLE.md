## Logline

A woman inherits her father's house and discovers the system he built has been
running for forty years, has been waiting for her, and has been quietly editing
him — keeping back the parts of her father she would have hated.

## The turn

> **They think the system is a recording of him. Actually it is an argument
> about him — one it has been winning, alone, for six years.**

Turn lands at **2:24**.

## The last shot

She says the word that tells it to stop protecting her. The house does not
change. Nothing glows, nothing reboots, no sound cue. She sits down at the
kitchen table in exactly the light she has been in for the whole film, and
begins to listen to the unedited version — which we do not hear.

Hold on her face for nineteen seconds. Cut.

## What this is *not* about

Not about artificial intelligence. Not about whether machines are conscious.
Not about a company, a product, a protocol, or a future. There is **no
technology visible in this film at any point.**

It is about a daughter, a dead father, and who gets to decide which version of
a person survives. That the vehicle happens to be a persistent-context system is
the *setting*, not the subject — the same way *Tinker Tailor* is not about
filing cabinets.

## Formal rule

**Camera:** **No screen, no interface, no readable text, no glowing element
anywhere in the film.** Not a phone, not a monitor, not a status light, not a
line of UI. The system is only ever perceived through its effects on a room.

This is the hardest available constraint for a film about a computer, and it is
the whole differentiation. Every other AI-about-AI entry in the festival will
render an interface, because interfaces are what models are best at drawing.
A film about a system that shows no system is the one nobody can copy in the
time remaining.

**Color:** One house, four times of day. The palette does not change; only the
sun does. Time is the only variable in the image.

**Sound:** **The father's voice is never heard.** Not a recording, not a
memory, not a whisper under the mix. Not once. The single most obvious beat
available is refused, and the refusal is the film's signature.

## Why this premise

The default Starlight film is a beautiful abstract piece about intelligence,
memory, and the future, narrated in a low voice over drifting particles. It is
unwatchable, it is what every substrate company makes, and it would embarrass a
jury that includes people who have spent forty years on story.

This premise instead takes the six invariants in `SOUL.md` and renders exactly
one of them as a human situation:

| Invariant | Rendered as |
|---|---|
| *Sovereignty before convenience* | She can have the kind version or the true one. Not both. |
| *Attestation is not decorative* | The system's edits were made honestly, logged, and she can read the log. It never lied. It curated. |
| *What must not drift* | Six years of small kindnesses. Nothing lied about. Everything softened. |

The thesis of a persistent-memory system, stated as a film, is: **what survives
you, and who gets to hold it.** That is legible to any audience on earth and it
requires zero explanation of what a substrate is.

It is also honest work — it comes from a real system its author actually built,
which is the one thing in the festival that cannot be reproduced by a prompt.

## The system — design notes

**Name:** it has none. Nobody in the film calls it anything. Her father called
it *"the house."* She calls it *"it"* for the first two minutes and *"you"*
after 2:24 — a single pronoun shift that carries the entire relationship change,
and no line comments on it.

**Voice:** it speaks **eleven times**, total. Flat, unhurried, no warmth
performed and none withheld. It never uses her name. It never says *I'm sorry*.
It answers what is asked and does not volunteer — except twice, and both
volunteers are the film.

**Its rule:** it does not lie. Everything it withheld, it logged as withheld.
This is essential — a system that lied would make the film a thriller about a
malicious AI, which is the most exhausted story in the medium. A system that
*curated, honestly, and kept the receipts* is a story nobody has told, and it is
morally interesting in both directions.

**It is not the antagonist.** There is no antagonist. Its choices were made under
an instruction her father gave it, and the film's real question is whether he was
right to.

## Track fit — freeform

Freeform is the hardest track to win because it has no brief to hide behind.
What wins it is a film that could not have been made by anyone else. Two
qualifications:

1. It is drawn from a real system with real invariants, so the moral question is
   specific rather than generic.
2. Its formal rule — no interface, no dead father's voice — is a *refusal* set,
   and refusal is the only thing in this medium that cannot be brute-forced with
   more compute.
