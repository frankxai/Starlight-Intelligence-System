## 1 — Curriculum Design

### Outcome anchor

- **L3 behavior change (on-job, observable):** In weekly 1:1s, managers will give at least one specific behavioral observation per direct report per week — *behavior + impact + ask*, not judgment or vague praise — observed via 1:1 sampling and direct-report sampling at 30/60/90 days.
- **L4 business outcome:** Q3 engagement-survey item "I receive specific feedback from my manager that helps me grow" rises by ≥0.5 points (5-point scale) vs. Q2 baseline; voluntary attrition among learners' direct reports drops vs. control cohort over the next two quarters.
- **Cohort:** People-managers, first- and second-line, mixed function.
- **Cohort size:** 12-16.
- **Duration:** 6 weeks active + 90-day measurement window.
- **Prerequisites:** none (intentionally; this is foundational).

### Capability map

| L3 sub-behavior | Required sub-skill | Prerequisite knowledge | Manager already has? |
|-----------------|---------------------|------------------------|------------------------|
| Observes specific behavior in 1:1 | Behavioral observation distinct from judgment | Behavior vs. judgment distinction | Most: partial |
| States behavior + impact + ask | Three-part feedback structure | Why structure matters; common failure modes | Most: no |
| Holds difficult conversations without flinching | Emotional regulation under social pressure | Why managers flinch (limbic activation) | Most: no — this is the bottleneck |
| Receives feedback on their feedback | Soliciting upward feedback from directs | Psychological safety mechanics | Some: partial |
| Calibrates feedback against rubric over time | Rubric internalization + drift correction | Rubric structure; common drifts | None: no |

### Module sequence

#### Module 1 — Behavior vs. judgment (Week 1, 90 min synchronous + 45 min self-paced)
- **L2 outcome:** Manager can convert any judgment ("she's careless") into behavior ("she submitted three of the last five reports with calculation errors") in real time.
- **Chunks (≤4):** (1) the distinction, (2) the failure modes of judgment-as-feedback, (3) the conversion drill, (4) the in-meeting catch.
- **Activity mix:** 25% concept / 50% drill / 25% pair role-play.
- **Encoding context:** managers practice using their own actual direct reports (anonymized in cohort sharing).
- **Module evaluation rubric (L2):** Given 5 judgment statements, manager converts ≥4 to specific behavioral observations under time pressure (60 sec each).
- **Manager's manager engagement:** week-0 brief — "your direct report (this manager) is learning to convert judgment into behavior; expect them to ask you to do the same."

#### Module 2 — Behavior + Impact + Ask structure (Week 2, 90 min synchronous + 45 min self-paced)
- **L2 outcome:** Manager delivers feedback in three-part structure under role-play pressure.
- **Chunks (≤4):** (1) the structure, (2) why each part matters (limbic / cortical encoding for the receiver), (3) what each part looks like wrong, (4) the in-meeting application.
- **Activity mix:** 20% concept / 60% role-play / 20% peer feedback.
- **Encoding context:** role-play scenarios mirror the manager's actual 1:1 settings — same vocabulary, same kinds of issues, same emotional load.
- **Module evaluation rubric (L2):** In role-play, manager delivers three-part feedback with rubric pass on ≥3 of 4 attempts.

#### Module 3 — The flinch, and what to do instead (Week 3, 120 min synchronous + 30 min self-paced)
- **L2 outcome:** Manager identifies their own flinch pattern (avoid-the-conversation, soften-into-vagueness, criticize-via-email, sandwich-collapse) and runs through it without retreat.
- **Chunks (≤4):** (1) the limbic mechanics — why flinch happens (amygdala activation under perceived social threat), (2) the four common patterns, (3) the regulation drill (name it, breathe, slow the next sentence), (4) the recovery move when you've already flinched.
- **Activity mix:** 15% concept / 70% high-stakes role-play with debrief / 15% self-coaching plan.
- **Encoding context:** role-plays with stakes — actor playing a defensive direct report; manager has to not flinch.
- **Module evaluation rubric (L2):** Manager identifies their flinch pattern + executes one regulation drill in role-play under observation; debrief surfaces self-awareness.
- **Note:** This module is the load-bearing one. Most "feedback training" skips it because it's uncomfortable. That's why most feedback training fails.

#### Module 4 — The 1:1 architecture (Week 4, 90 min synchronous + 60 min on-job application)
- **L2 outcome:** Manager redesigns their own 1:1 cadence to make weekly behavioral feedback structural — not aspirational.
- **Chunks (≤4):** (1) what a high-feedback 1:1 looks like, (2) the manager's 1:1 audit (current state), (3) the redesign, (4) the weekly cadence + retrieval schedule.
- **Activity mix:** 20% concept / 60% audit-and-redesign / 20% peer review.
- **Encoding context:** managers redesign their *actual* 1:1 templates and schedules.
- **Module evaluation rubric (L2):** Manager submits redesigned 1:1 template with observable behavioral-feedback slot; peer review passes.

#### Module 5 — On-job application + R2 retrieval (Week 5, async + 30 min cohort retrieval)
- **L2 outcome:** Manager has run two weeks of redesigned 1:1s with at least one behavioral observation per direct report per week.
- **Activity mix:** 100% on-job + cohort retrieval.
- **Encoding context:** real 1:1s with real direct reports.
- **Module evaluation rubric (L3-early):** Self-report with three behavioral examples; peer-pair retrieval discussion surfaces patterns.

#### Module 6 — Calibration + close (Week 6, 90 min synchronous)
- **L2 outcome:** Manager calibrates their own feedback against the rubric; identifies their drift patterns.
- **Chunks (≤4):** (1) what drift looks like, (2) calibration with peer + facilitator, (3) the 90-day plan, (4) the manager's own commitment artifact.
- **Activity mix:** 30% calibration / 30% peer review of recordings / 40% commitment + reinforcement plan.
- **Encoding context:** managers bring real (anonymized) feedback they gave; cohort calibrates.
- **Module evaluation rubric (L2):** Calibration pass against rubric; commitment artifact completed.

### Trainer brief (selected highlights)

**Voice & tone:** facilitator-as-coach, not facilitator-as-expert. Hold silence. Ask questions. Refuse to lecture. The cohort is full of senior managers; they will pattern-match to "training" and try to switch into student mode. Don't let them. Force them to do the work.

**Per-module talking points (M3 example):**
- Open with: "Today is the module that most feedback training skips. We're not going to skip it. Take a breath."
- Frame the limbic mechanics in 8 minutes maximum — managers already know what fear feels like; they don't need a 30-min lecture on it.
- Spend the rest of the time in role-play with stakes and debrief.
- The actor's job is to be defensive in a realistic way. The manager's job is to not flinch. Both are coached afterward.

**Common learner failure modes:**
- *Lecture-the-direct-report-back* — manager defaults to explaining the behavior + impact at length; loses the "ask" and the conversation. Coach: cut the explanation by half.
- *Sandwich-collapse* — manager softens the message between two pieces of vague praise, undermining the signal. Coach: drop the bread; the meat alone is the whole sandwich.
- *Email-after-the-fact* — manager flinches in the meeting, then sends a feedback email. Coach: this is the avoidance pattern in disguise; address in M3.
- *Vague-into-behavior* — manager *says* "let me give you behavioral feedback" then proceeds to give judgment. Coach: catch in real-time; ask "what was the specific observable?"

**Scenario answer keys:** see `hr-intelligence/training/perf-conversations-mastery/scenarios-feedback-conversations-2026-04-24.md`.

### Manager-of-Manager engagement plan (mandatory)

#### Pre-program brief (1 page)

> **What your direct reports (the managers in this program) are learning:** to give specific behavioral feedback in 1:1s, weekly, using the structure: behavior + impact + ask.
>
> **What you should expect to observe at 30/60/90 days:**
> - 30 days: managers in your team mention specific behavior in their 1:1 prep with you ("I want to give X feedback this week — here's what I'm going to say").
> - 60 days: managers' direct reports start spontaneously thanking them for specific feedback (you may hear about this from skip-levels).
> - 90 days: in your skip-level meetings, direct reports describe their feedback as specific and useful — vs. baseline of "vague" or "I don't really get feedback."
>
> **What to reinforce when you see it:** "I noticed you brought a specific behavioral observation to your last 1:1 with [direct report]. That's exactly the kind of feedback that compounds." Specific, behavioral, brief.
>
> **What to coach when it slips:** When you hear "she's struggling" or "he's not engaged" from your manager — ask: "What specific behavior have you observed?" Don't lecture; just ask the question. Repeat as many times as needed.

#### In-program checkpoints
- Manager-of-Manager observes 1 1:1 in week 1, 1 1:1 in week 3 (after M3).
- Weekly 5-minute check-in with the manager: "what are you trying from training this week?"
- Reviews redesigned 1:1 template after M4.
- Reviews commitment artifact after M6.

#### Post-program reinforcement protocol
- 30 days: L3 observation cycle 1 against rubric. Coaching conversation if drift.
- 60 days: L3 observation cycle 2. Track delta from 30-day.
- 90 days: L3 observation cycle 3. Inputs to L4 metric review.

- **Self-awareness of failure modes:** some — knows they have a tendency to over-explain
- **Risk profile for cohort 2:** medium — pair with Ana as observer/coach for cohort 2; SME solo for cohort 3

### TtT curriculum (6 sessions, 4 weeks before cohort 2 delivery)

#### Session 1 — Adult learning principles (90 min)
- Knowles condensed; SME redesigns M1 from "lecture" to "drill" frame and gets coached.

#### Session 2 — Memory and transfer mechanics (90 min)
- Sweller / Roediger-Karpicke / Tulving applied to this specific program. SME audits M3 cognitive load.

#### Session 3 — Facilitation skills (120 min)
- Holding silence, asking instead of answering, running role-play with stakes, debriefing without lecturing. Live drills.

#### Session 4 — SME-as-trainer failure modes (90 min)
- SME watches recordings of common patterns. Self-identifies their lecture-collapse tendency. Builds self-coaching plan.

#### Session 5 — Live cohort facilitation practice (180 min)
- SME facilitates M3 (the hardest module) with practice cohort of 8 peers. Recorded. Ana observes silently.
- Detailed behavioral coaching feedback after.

#### Session 6 — Final readiness check + program-specific brief (90 min)
- Rubric pass with Ana.
- Mastered scenario answer keys, prepared learner-failure-mode responses, debrief discussion prompts internalized.

### Scenarios (selected — full library has all 14)

#### 1. "She's careless" — converting judgment to behavior — intro

**Sub-skill:** behavior vs. judgment

**Setup:**
You are a finance manager. You're prepping for a 1:1 with Marta, a senior analyst on your team. You've been frustrated. In your prep notes you wrote: "Marta is being careless this quarter." You want to give her feedback, and you know "you're being careless" is not feedback. You have 5 minutes to convert your prep into something specific and behavioral.

**Decision point:**
Convert "Marta is being careless" into 1-3 specific behavioral observations you could deliver, with impact and ask.

**Response rubric:**
- **Strong response includes:** specific instance(s) with date or week, observable behavior (e.g., "the Q2 board deck submitted Monday had three of the seven figure totals incorrect on slide 12"), impact ("this required a re-review with the CFO and pushed our Tuesday meeting"), ask ("can you walk me through what your review checklist looks like before submission?").
- **Weak response misses:** still uses character labels ("she's not detail-oriented"); generalizes ("recently you've been making mistakes"); skips the ask.
- **Common traps:** frames the ask as a directive ("you need to be more careful") rather than as inquiry; conflates behavior with personality.

**Debrief discussion guide:**
- Q1: What did you notice when you tried to make it specific?
- Q2: Why does the ask matter as much as the observation?
- Q3: Where else in your team does "careless" or similar judgment-as-feedback show up?
- Hold silence. Ask, don't tell.

#### 7. The emotional reaction — mid

**Sub-skill:** regulation under social pressure (M3)

**Setup:**
You are a product manager. You're giving Liam, a senior engineer, feedback about his behavior in a recent design review (he interrupted a PM colleague three times in the meeting and the colleague stopped contributing). You deliver the feedback in three-part structure: behavior, impact, ask. Halfway through, Liam's eyes start to redden. He says, quietly: "I don't think you understand how hard this quarter has been for me."

**Decision points:**
1. What do you say in the next 15 seconds?
2. Do you continue with the original message, pivot to the personal moment, or close the loop later?

**Response rubric:**
- **Strong response includes:** acknowledges the emotional content ("I can see this landed hard, and I do want to know what's been hard"), holds the original message ("and the behavior we're talking about is still important to address"), offers either a pause now or a return later, *names the structure of the choice* so Liam doesn't feel ambushed.
- **Weak response misses:** retracts the feedback ("oh, sorry, never mind, we can talk later" — the avoidance pattern); ignores the emotion and barrels through ("I hear you, but the issue is..."); collapses into therapy-mode ("tell me everything about your quarter" — feedback-now becomes feedback-never).
- **Common traps:** the manager flinches into care-mode and the message dies; or the manager hardens into compliance-mode and the relationship dies. The middle path holds both.

**Debrief discussion guide:**
- Q1: What did your body do when Liam's eyes reddened?
- Q2: What is the difference between holding care AND holding the message vs. having to choose one?
- Q3: When have you seen this go badly — from either side?
- Hold silence longer than feels comfortable. The cohort will fill it; your job is not to fill it.

---

#### 9. Cross-cultural directness mismatch — advanced

**Sub-skill:** culturally adaptive feedback while preserving signal (M3 + R3 cohort)

**Setup:**
You are a Dutch manager (you grew up giving and receiving directness as the norm). You manage Hiroki, a senior engineer who joined your team six months ago from a Tokyo office. You've given Hiroki feedback the way you give everyone feedback: behavior + impact + ask, direct, in the 1:1. After the third such conversation, Hiroki's manager-from-Tokyo (your peer) reaches out: "Hiroki feels you've been highly critical. He's considering leaving. He thought your feedback was a final warning." You believed you were giving constructive feedback at the level you give everyone.

**Decision points:**
1. Do you change how you give Hiroki feedback?
2. Do you change how you give *everyone* feedback?
3. What do you say in your next 1:1 with Hiroki?

**Response rubric:**
- **Strong response includes:** distinguishes content (the behavior + impact + ask is the same) from delivery context (signaling, framing, register can adapt); names the meta-level explicitly with Hiroki ("I want to check how my feedback has been landing for you — I deliver it in a register that comes from how I learned, and I want to know what's reading as critical that I intend as developmental"); does not retract the substantive feedback; does not collapse into uniform-soft mode.
- **Weak response misses:** retracts the feedback ("oh sorry I won't do that anymore"); softens to the point of unintelligibility; or doubles down on directness with a "this is how I am" frame that ignores the asymmetry; treats this as Hiroki's problem to adapt rather than a shared problem.
- **Common traps:** "I'll just be softer with him" — creates a two-tier system that becomes unworkable; "this is how I am" — abdicates the relational responsibility; collapsing the substantive feedback to preserve the relationship — the relationship survives but the feedback dies.

**Debrief discussion guide:**
- Q1: What did the manager get right? What did they miss?
- Q2: How do you distinguish "register" from "content" in feedback?
- Q3: When have you encountered this asymmetry in your own teams? What worked?
- Q4: What would the conversation with Hiroki look like — say it out loud?
- This is the longest debrief. Hold the cohort in the discomfort.
